### Post 04 — Day 19, Sat 7:00pm ET
**Type:** Founder reflection (Sunday reflection slot)
**Title:** Why I stopped writing proposals on Sundays.

For a long time I treated Sunday night as my "catch up on proposals" slot. I'd come back to a Monday inbox of seven RFPs and feel like I was getting ahead.

I was not getting ahead. I was teaching prospects that I responded fastest at 9pm on Sunday, which selected for the worst prospects — the ones who wanted vendors to be on-call.

I now have a hard rule. Proposals get written between 10am and 4pm on weekdays. Discovery calls happen on Tuesday-Thursday. Anyone who needs same-day on a Sunday is buying a different product than I'm selling.

This sounds like a boundaries thing. It's actually a margins thing. The proposals I wrote on Sundays converted at 18%. The proposals I write on weekdays convert at over 40%. Same words. Different signals.

Self-selection is one of the highest-leverage things a small firm can do. Your hours of availability are part of your product.

### Post 06 — Day 35, Mon 7:45am ET
**Type:** Founder reflection
**Title:** The pricing mistake every consultant makes once.

I priced my first three projects too low. By a lot.

The reason wasn't impostor syndrome. The reason was that I priced based on what I would have charged at my last salaried job, plus a freelance premium. That math is wrong.

A salaried developer's hourly cost to an employer is roughly 1.4x base salary. A consultant's effective hourly cost has to cover: time spent prospecting (real), time spent writing proposals that don't win (real), time spent on bookkeeping, taxes, software, hardware, sick days, and the months between projects.

The number I now use is 3x the salaried-equivalent hourly rate. Not because I'm greedy. Because if I bill less than that, I'm subsidizing the client's project out of my retirement.

Most operators will pay 3x once they understand the math. The ones who won't pay 3x were going to be a billing dispute anyway.

If you're underpricing right now, raising your rate tomorrow is not aggressive. It's accurate.

### Post 10 — Day 86, Wed 8:00am ET

**Title:** Three things I changed about my consulting practice in 2026.

One: I stopped doing free discovery calls longer than 25 minutes. The 60-minute "tell me about your problem" call is a contract for free consulting. Now I do 25 minutes, structured, and quote at the end.

Two: I stopped sending PDFs as proposals. Proposals now live on a private URL with a click-to-accept link. I get the timestamp of every view, which tells me when to follow up. Conversion went from a guess to a number.

Three: I stopped quoting hourly rates on retainers. Retainers now quote outcomes-per-month with a flat fee. Hourly rates trained clients to count my minutes. Flat outcome fees trained them to count their wins.

None of these were big strategic shifts. They were small mechanical changes that compounded into a much better quarter.

If you're a consultant heading into your next year, look at the small mechanics, not the big strategy. The mechanics are where the leverage is.

## Industry Hot Takes — 2

### Post 24 — Day 17, Thu 7:45am ET
**Type:** Industry hot take
**Title:** Most "AI-powered" tools sold to SMBs in 2026 are GPT-4 wrapped around a feature you already had.

I will say this in public: the AI tooling market for small and mid-market businesses is currently 60% repackaged LLM wrappers and 40% legitimate workflow improvements. The hard part is telling them apart before you've spent six months on a subscription.

Here's the test I use. Ask the vendor: what is your pipeline before the prompt? Not what model are you using. Not what fine-tuning. What does the input look like before the LLM ever sees it? If the answer is "the user uploads a CSV," or "the user pastes text," you're buying ChatGPT with a different UI.

The legitimate workflow tools have real pipeline work — Google Places ingestion, web scraping with validation rules, document parsing, OCR, structured extraction — and the LLM is the last 10% of the workflow, not the first.

I'm not saying don't buy AI tools. I'm saying ask the pipeline question before you sign.

**CTA:** What's a tool you bought that turned out to be a ChatGPT skin? No naming-and-shaming required.

### Post 25 — Day 64, Mon 7:45am ET
**Type:** Industry hot take
**Title:** The death of the "agency" model is exaggerated. The death of the bad-agency model is not.

There's a recurring thread on LinkedIn about how AI is killing the agency model. It isn't. It's killing the bad agency model — the one that staffed 18 people to deliver 6 people's worth of output, billed time-and-materials with no commitment to outcomes, and called the work done at handoff.

The agency model that lives is the one with a tight team, clear outcomes-per-engagement, a writeable case study at the end, and a retainer that prices on results rather than hours. That model isn't getting killed by AI. It's getting amplified by it.

If you run a small studio or consultancy in 2026 and you're worried about being replaced, the right move isn't to compete with the AI tools. It's to be the operator who knows which AI tools to use, which to skip, and how to integrate them into a delivery system that ships on time.

The agencies that die in 2026 were the ones that were going to die in 2027 anyway.
