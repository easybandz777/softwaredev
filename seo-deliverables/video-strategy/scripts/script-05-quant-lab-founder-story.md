My name is Bill Beltz. I run a custom software and cybersecurity firm out of Macon, Georgia, called QUANT LAB USA. ## [00:25 — The "why" origin, 0:25 to 1:40]

I started QUANT LAB at the end of 2024. Before that I had been writing software for years — production systems, integrations, the unglamorous stuff that actually runs businesses. I worked across enough projects to notice the same pattern over and over again.

Small and mid-sized businesses kept ending up in one of two places. They were either paying a SaaS vendor for software that almost fit their workflow but forced them to bend their process around it. Or they had hired an agency to build something custom and gotten a six-figure invoice for software that did not match what they actually needed — because the people who took the meetings were not the people who wrote the code, and three layers of telephone-game later, the build came out crooked.

I kept thinking: there is a missing tier in the market. Between "rent SaaS from a public company" and "spend $400k with an agency," there should be a tier that says: one experienced engineer, working directly with the business owner, building the thing the business owner actually described, in a timeframe that does not require three rounds of equity financing.

So I built that tier. That is the entire pitch.

## [01:40 — Why Macon, 1:40 to 2:30]

Why Macon, Georgia? Three reasons.

First, the cost structure works. I do not pay San Francisco rent. I do not have a Manhattan office. That savings goes into my pricing — every project I bill comes in below what a coastal agency would charge, and I still take home a salary that lets me sleep at night.

Second, the time zone is friendly. I am Eastern, which means I overlap with US clients from Boston to LA without anyone having to do a 6 a.m. call. I have shipped work for clients in Seattle, Miami, and New York from the same desk.

Third — and this matters more than people admit — I have no commute and no in-office politics. When a client emails me at 2pm on a Tuesday, I am at the keyboard. When something breaks in production, I am the one who fixes it, not a ticket assigned to a queue.

## [02:30 — What I build, 2:30 to 3:45]

What I actually build, in order of how often clients ask for it:

**Custom CRMs and operations software.** A construction firm with a job-tracking workflow that does not fit any off-the-shelf CRM. A medical billing operation that needs claims pipeline visibility. A motorcycle shop that needs POS plus service tickets plus inventory in one screen. I have shipped fourteen of these. Each is built to match how the business actually runs, not how a SaaS vendor thinks it should run.

**Stripe and payments work.** Subscription billing, licensing servers, invoicing platforms. Stripe's docs make a "hello world" integration look easy. Production Stripe — handling webhooks, refunds, currency, tax, disputes, and metered billing — is a separate skill. I do that work.

**Penetration testing.** This is the cybersecurity side. Web application pentest, network pentest, wireless pentest, active directory pentest. All aligned to the MITRE ATT&CK framework, which is the standard real security teams use. I have a video coming on what pentests actually cost, because that is the question I get every week.

**Algorithmic trading systems.** This is the niche-y one. I have built a multi-strategy trading desk, an order router, and a few research-environment systems for serious quants. If you are running real capital and you need infrastructure that holds up, this is in scope.

## [03:45 — The fourteen-client filter, 3:45 to 4:40]

Why fourteen clients matters: it is enough to have a pattern, and small enough that every one is real.

If a firm tells you they have done four hundred CRM implementations, they are running a template. There is no other way the math works. Four hundred CRMs is a factory.

Fourteen clients in roughly a year is the number you get when each project is custom-scoped, each one has the founder writing the code, and each one ships. That is the velocity of one experienced engineer working at full capacity. It is not a brag. It is just the math.

The case studies are all on quantlabusa.dev/work. Names, industries, before and after, the technical stack. If you want to see the receipts before you reach out, that is the first place to go.

## [04:40 — How to work with me, 4:40 to 5:30]

If you are considering hiring me, here is how it goes.

You start at quantlabusa.dev/contact and tell me what you are trying to build. I respond within one business day, almost always within the same day. We get on a call, free of charge, no obligation, and I tell you honestly whether what you need is something I can build, something you should buy off the shelf, or something that does not exist yet and would cost too much to invent.

If we move forward, I write a scope and a fixed price. You see what you are paying for and what you are getting before you sign anything. There is no hourly billing creep. There is no scope ambiguity.

If we do not move forward, I will still point you to whatever vendor or solution I would pick if I were in your seat. That advice is free.

## [05:30 — Soft close, 5:30 to 6:30]

That is the firm. One person, fourteen engagements, three service lines, in Macon, Georgia.

If you are watching this and you have a software problem in your business that has been sitting on the back burner for six months because nobody has given you a straight answer about it — quantlabusa.dev/contact. I will give you a straight answer. The worst that happens is you waste twenty minutes of your week.

Thanks for watching.
